    Tutorial 0

    Getting started with Conatus

    One problem from beginning to end: a layer of fluid heated from below. You will write its equations as on paper, ask whether the fluid at rest is stable, find where convection starts to the digits of the published value, then simulate the rolls that appear above onset and measure the heat they carry. About half an hour, on a laptop.

    Everything below is one Python script, shown piece by piece. It ran as shown, and each output is what it printed. The whole script is in the documentation; run it with python t00_getting_started.py.

    1. Install

    Conatus needs Linux x86-64 and Python 3.11, 3.12 or 3.13 (3.12 recommended); the wheel carries its compiled core, so no compiler is needed. The tutorials extra adds matplotlib, which draws this tutorial's figure. The last line checks the installation and says what this machine can run: the compiled core, a GPU.

    For a GPU, see Install: the CUDA 12 wheel (beta) of the same release.

    2. Write the model

    Rayleigh–Bénard convection is the flow of a fluid layer between a hot plate below and a cold plate above. Warm fluid is lighter and wants to rise; viscosity and heat diffusion hold it back. In the Boussinesq approximation, with lengths in units of the layer depth and times in units of the thermal diffusion time, the velocity u, the pressure p and the temperature T obey

    ∂tu + u·∇u = −∇p + Pr ∇²u + Pr Ra T ez

    ∇·u = 0

    ∂tT + u·∇T = ∇²T

    u = 0 and T = ±½ on the plates z = ∓½

    Two numbers set the physics: the Rayleigh number Ra, the strength of the heating against the two diffusions, and the Prandtl number Pr, the ratio of viscosity to heat diffusivity. In Conatus the model is these lines:

    Read it as you would read the equations. cn.Box declares the domain: periodic in x with length 2 (one pair of counter-rotating rolls), between walls at z = ±½. The domain names its boundaries, bottom and top. The model declares its unknowns (a velocity, a pressure, a scalar) and its parameters, then the equations in a string, written as on paper: u·grad(u) is the advection, ez the vertical unit vector. The conditions are stated where they hold. There is no basis to choose, no resolution yet, and nothing to say about how the boundary conditions are imposed.

    3. Read what Conatus decided

    Some decisions are needed to turn the model into a computation. Conatus makes them by fixed rules, and explain() lists every one. A few lines of its report:

    Linear terms are treated implicitly and nonlinear ones explicitly; m.explain(full=True) lists every term with the side it went to and why. Each boundary condition is attached to the equation where its unknown has its highest derivative across the walls. The pressure is recognised from the structure of the equations, not from its name, and its free constant is fixed. Dealiasing uses the standard 3/2 rule, said aloud. Anything the rules cannot decide is refused with a message that says what to write instead: nothing is guessed.

    4. Is the fluid at rest stable?

    With the plates at fixed temperatures, the fluid can stay at rest while heat simply conducts through it: T = −z, u = 0. Whether it does depends on Ra. Ask the model for the normal modes of small disturbances to that state, for the rolls that fit the box (wavenumber k = π):

    linearize derives the linear equations from the ones you wrote; eigenvalues solves them with 24 Chebyshev modes across the layer. Here you choose the resolution: Conatus never does. At Ra = 1500 every disturbance decays; at Ra = 2500 one grows. Convection starts in between.

    5. Where convection starts

    To find the threshold, write the problem as it appears in textbooks. A disturbance of wavenumber k behaves as eikx + st; its vertical velocity w(z) and temperature θ(z) satisfy two ordinary differential equations in which s is the eigenvalue. This is a model on a line, with the eigenvalue declared:

    parametric keeps Ra and k as live parameters of the discretised problem; critical finds the Ra at which the least stable mode stops decaying, and minimises it over k. The filter abs(s) < 100 is a declared choice: it sets aside the spurious modes that boundary conditions add to any polynomial discretisation, far out in the spectrum. The result is the classical onset of convection between rigid plates, Rac = 1707.762 at kc = 3.117 (Reid & Harris 1958; Chandrasekhar 1961), to every published digit.

    6. Simulate above onset

    Back to the full equations, at Ra = 2500. A simulation needs a resolution, a time-stepping scheme and a time step: three arguments, never defaults. Start from the conduction state with a tiny roll, and measure how fast it grows:

    The simulation and the eigenvalue problem are two different computations from the same model; while the disturbance is small they must agree, and they do, to the sixth digit. run(until=...) refuses a time that is not a whole number of steps rather than change the step behind your back.

    7. Steady rolls

    Let the rolls grow until the nonlinear terms stop them. The run is recorded in a registry as it goes (next section). Then measure the Nusselt number Nu, the heat carried across the layer in units of what conduction alone would carry, three ways: averaged over the layer, and through each plate.

    In a steady state the heat that enters through the hot plate leaves through the cold one, and crosses every level in between: the three numbers agree. The rolls carry about half as much heat again as conduction.

    8. The Methods paragraph and the run

    Every simulation can describe itself: sim.methods() writes the paragraph of a paper that says how the result was computed, with its references. Its opening:

    The IVP 'Rayleigh-Benard' for (u, p, T) was solved with Conatus. The domain is discretized as follows: x ∈ [0, 2) is periodic and expanded in a real (cosine/sine) Fourier series of 32 modes; z ∈ [-0.5, 0.5] is expanded in 24 Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind. Derivatives, basis conversions and multiplications by non-constant coefficients use the sparse, banded ultraspherical operators of Olver & Townsend, and boundary conditions are imposed with the generalized tau method (4 tau variable(s), each entering through a lift polynomial), following Burns et al. …

    The with cn.runs.track(...) block above kept the run in a registry (a folder, here runs): the problem, the numerics, the parameters, the code version, the start and end states, and the results you attach. Later, cn.runs.find(Nu__gt=1.4) finds it again, compare shows what differs between two runs, and reproduce checks a rerun bit for bit. See the run registry.

    10. The checks

    The script ends by checking itself against the literature and against the identities above. If Conatus ever stopped reproducing them, the script would fail: the outputs on this page come from its last execution, which passed.

    11. What next

    Things to try with this script: raise Ra and measure how Nu grows (a faster flow may need a smaller time step: sim.cfl() suggests one, it never imposes one); change Pr; make the box longer, cn.periodic(4.0), and see which roll pair grows first. Other domains, a disk or a spherical shell, are one line each: see Geometries and, for shells, the user guide. Then tutorials 1 to 14, each a short script ending on a published result:

    1. A first eigenvalue problem: Orr–Sommerfeld: a native model, conditions by wall name, eigenvalues with residuals, critical parameters, verify
    2. A first initial-value problem: Rayleigh–Bénard 2D, CPU then GPU: a simulation on CPU, then on GPU; time steppers, CFL, the *Methods* paragraph
    3. Symmetries and symmetry subspaces: linearizing a native model, symmetry subspaces: the same eigenvalue with half the unknowns
    4. Nonlinear boundary-value problems and continuation: Bratu: a nonlinear boundary-value model, Newton on the exact Jacobian, pseudo-arclength continuation, folds
    5. Invariant solutions: Kuramoto–Sivashinsky, L = 22: equilibria, travelling waves and periodic orbits of a native model by Newton–Krylov–hookstep, Floquet exponents
    6. Polar geometry: a drum, a pipe, two rotating cylinders: polar and cylindrical geometry: a drum on cn.Disk, ready pipe and Taylor–Couette problems
    7. Bifurcations: branch switching, cycles, codimension 2, normal forms: branch switching, cycles, codimension 2, normal forms, Landau and Ginzburg–Landau
    8. Record, query, reproduce: the run registry: the run registry on a native simulation: record, certify, query, compare, reproduce bit for bit, cache only on request
    9. Chaos and ensembles: Lyapunov spectrum, covariant vectors, Poincaré map, basins: Lyapunov spectrum, covariant vectors, Kaplan–Yorke, Poincaré map, basins from one ensemble
    10. Where a global mode is born, and what the data say about it: direct and adjoint global modes, wavemaker, base-flow sensitivity; DMD in the exact norm
    11. Coming from Dedalus: a convection cell as a native model, then read from a Dedalus script: line-by-line report, run, native vs translated; optional check against Dedalus
    12. Covariant Lyapunov vectors of Kuramoto–Sivashinsky: tangencies and the physical modes: sim.lyapunov with covariant vectors: exponents with errors, covariance residual, tangencies, domination and the physical modes
    13. Lagrangian tracers and a numerical dye: sim.particles and m.dye: exact velocity at the tracers, observed order in time, a dye whose mass is measured

    Every part of Conatus is described once in the documentation: the user guide, the API reference and the mathematics behind each method.
